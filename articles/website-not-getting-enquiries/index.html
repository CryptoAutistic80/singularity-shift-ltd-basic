Why Is Your Website Not Getting Enquiries? A Cheltenham Business Checklist

Work out whether low website enquiries come from visibility, unclear content or a broken contact route, with practical checks for Cheltenham businesses.

By James Walford · Website designer & software developer, Cheltenham

4 min read · For: Cheltenham business owners receiving fewer useful website enquiries than expected.

In this guide

  1. Make sure an enquiry can reach you
  2. Separate being found from persuading a visitor
  3. Read the service page as a new customer
  4. Give each page a sensible next action
  5. Example: enquiries that are mostly the wrong fit
  6. Change one clear problem at a time

A quiet inbox does not tell you whether your website needs more traffic, clearer content or a repaired contact form. Those are different problems. Changing colours or buying more visitors before checking the enquiry route can spend money without addressing either.

If you run a business in Cheltenham, begin with one service you genuinely want more work for. Follow the path a suitable customer would take from finding that service to contacting you. Keep notes on where the information or the process becomes uncertain.

Make sure an enquiry can reach you

Send a clearly labelled test message from the actual public website. Do this on a phone as well as a larger screen, and use an external email address if you can. Check the destination inbox, spam folder and any dashboard supplied by the form provider.

Seeing a thank-you message is not proof that the notification arrived. Equally, a missing email does not always mean the message was lost if the form service stores it elsewhere. Identify which part succeeded before changing the form.

Check any telephone and email links too. An outdated number, mistyped address or button leading to an old page is a concrete defect to fix before evaluating the design.

Separate being found from persuading a visitor

Review evidence in stages. Are the relevant pages appearing in search results? Are people clicking them? Are visitors reaching the enquiry route? Are the enquiries suitable for the work you offer?

Google Search Console can help with the search stage, but it does not replace checking your contact process. Our local search website checklist explains where the website and Google Business Profile each fit.

Small numbers require care. One quiet week is not enough to prove a redesign failed, and one busy day does not establish that a headline caused the increase. Compare a sensible period with similar trading conditions and record any advertising, seasonal or service changes.

Read the service page as a new customer

Someone finding you for the first time has none of the context you carry as the owner. A headline such as "Quality solutions delivered with care" says little about what they can buy.

Before asking for an enquiry, make the practical offer clear:

  • What specific work do you undertake?
  • Who is the service suitable for?
  • Do you serve the customer's location?
  • What information do you need to assess the job?
  • What happens after someone contacts you?
  • What real evidence helps them judge your work?

Use photographs, examples and feedback only where they are genuine and you have permission to publish them. If the business is new, explaining the process honestly is more useful than manufacturing the appearance of a long customer history.

Give each page a sensible next action

A visitor comparing options may need to see examples before sending a message. Someone with a broken website may be ready to describe the fault. The next action should fit the page's purpose.

Use specific wording such as "Tell me about your project" or "Ask about availability" where it accurately describes the response. Avoid putting several equally prominent actions together without explaining their differences.

Keep the form proportionate. Ask for information needed to make the next decision, rather than a complete specification before a conversation. Where detailed information is necessary, explain why.

W3C guidance recommends clear form labels and instructions so people can understand what each control needs. Visible labels also remain useful after someone starts typing. W3C guidance on labelling form controls.

Example: enquiries that are mostly the wrong fit

Imagine a Cheltenham decorator receiving requests for work outside the area they serve. This is a hypothetical example, not a result achieved for a client.

Adding more traffic may increase that problem. A better first step would state the actual service area, show representative work and explain what to include in an enquiry. A project-location field could help route the conversation without turning the form into an obstacle.

The business could then compare the proportion of suitable enquiries over time. The aim is useful conversations, not merely a larger message count.

Change one clear problem at a time

Begin with defects, then information gaps, then presentation experiments. Fixing an undelivered message has a direct explanation. A broad redesign changes many things together and makes it harder to understand what mattered.

Keep a short log of the page changed, the date, the reason and the evidence you will review. Check the complete enquiry route again after every relevant change. If the page is awkward on a phone, the mobile-friendly website guide provides a practical test sequence.
