A Local Search Website Checklist for Cheltenham Businesses

A practical Cheltenham local search checklist covering useful service pages, Google Business Profile, indexing checks and honest ways to measure progress.

By James Walford · Website designer & software developer, Cheltenham

4 min read · For: Cheltenham businesses improving how customers find and assess them online.

In this guide

  1. Understand the website and Business Profile separately
  2. Represent your location honestly
  3. Give important services enough explanation
  4. Check what Google actually knows about a page
  5. Example: one service page that needs more substance
  6. Measure relevant discovery and useful enquiries

Being found locally involves more than placing "Cheltenham" in every heading. A potential customer needs to find a relevant result, understand what you provide and feel confident enough to contact you. Your website and your business information should support that whole journey.

This checklist helps a Cheltenham business identify useful improvements before paying for a large batch of pages or a promise of first place. It separates the information you can improve from the decisions Google makes about displaying results.

Understand the website and Business Profile separately

Your website is where you can explain services, answer detailed questions and present evidence of your work. Google Business Profile represents eligible businesses in features such as Maps and local results. Improving one does not automatically fix the other.

Google describes local ranking in terms of relevance, distance and prominence. Accurate information helps Google understand a business, but there is no option to buy or request a better local ranking. Google's local ranking guidance.

Check your name, category, contact details, opening information and website link where applicable. Use the real business name rather than adding a string of search phrases to it.

Represent your location honestly

A Cheltenham address should describe a genuine business location. Do not present a borrowed address or a location page as evidence of an office that does not exist.

Google's eligibility and representation rules distinguish customer-facing premises from service-area businesses. An eligible business that visits customers should use its real service area and hide an address where customers are not served. Online-only businesses are generally ineligible for a Business Profile. Google Business Profile guidelines.

On the website, explain where you work in ordinary language. If you travel beyond Cheltenham, tell customers which areas you actually cover and whether distance affects availability. That is useful information without inventing a separate branch for every nearby town.

Give important services enough explanation

Start with the service a customer is trying to buy. A useful page answers what is included, who it suits, what the process involves and how to take the next step.

Write a clear page title and main heading. Link to the page from relevant navigation and related content. Add genuine examples or photographs where they help someone evaluate the work.

There is little value in publishing ten pages that differ only in the town name. Google's spam policies specifically address doorway pages and large amounts of content created primarily to manipulate rankings without adding value. Google Search spam policies.

Distinct questions can justify distinct guides. A customer choosing a booking system needs different advice from one comparing website maintenance options. Let the question determine the page, rather than multiplying near-identical phrases.

Check what Google actually knows about a page

Use Search Console's URL Inspection for an important service page. The indexed report describes Google's stored information; the live test checks the current page's potential indexability. A successful live fetch does not itself prove the page has been indexed or will rank. Google's URL Inspection documentation.

Record the exact result and date. Ask a developer to investigate specific failures, such as an inaccessible page or an unintended indexing restriction. Ensure the sitemap reflects the pages you want discovered and that those pages also have useful internal links.

Do not assume every delay is a technical defect. Equally, do not dismiss a clear fetch or server error as something that will fix itself. The reported evidence should determine the next action.

Example: one service page that needs more substance

Imagine a Cheltenham furniture restorer whose website says only "Restoration services available." This is a hypothetical example.

A more useful page could explain the types of furniture assessed, show permitted photographs of real work, describe how to request an initial assessment and clarify collection arrangements. A related guide could explain which photographs help the restorer understand a damaged piece.

Those pages answer different customer questions. Repeating the same vague paragraph across neighbourhood pages would not provide the same help.

Measure relevant discovery and useful enquiries

Review which search queries and pages generate impressions and clicks, then compare those patterns with the enquiries you receive. Keep business-name searches separate in your thinking from people who are discovering you for the first time.

Record meaningful changes and allow enough data to compare similar periods. Search positions vary by query and context; no website can guarantee reaching everyone who searches for a developer or another local service.

If visitors arrive but do not contact you, use the website enquiry checklist to examine the next stage. Discovery and enquiry quality need attention together.
