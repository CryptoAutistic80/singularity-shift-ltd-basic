# When Spreadsheets Start Getting in the Way: A Practical Guide for Growing Businesses

Spreadsheets are useful. They are quick to start, familiar to most teams and often exactly right for an early calculation, list or report.

The trouble begins when a spreadsheet quietly becomes the system that runs an important part of the business.

If it controls customer information, pricing, bookings, inventory, approvals or the handover between people, the spreadsheet is no longer just a spreadsheet. It is an operational system—usually without clear permissions, reliable history, validation or a safe way to handle growth.

That does not mean every spreadsheet needs replacing. It means the business should decide which parts of the work are genuinely becoming risky or expensive to manage manually.

## Signs the current process has outgrown its tools

Look for patterns rather than one-off mistakes.

- The same information is copied between a spreadsheet, email, a CRM and another tool.
- People ask who has the latest version.
- Important calculations depend on formulas that only one person understands.
- A small change creates a chain of manual updates.
- Customers or colleagues have to wait for someone to find information for them.
- Errors are found after a decision has been made or a customer has been contacted.
- The process works only because a particular person remembers every exception.

These are not failures of the people doing the work. They are signs that the process deserves better support.

## Do not replace everything at once

The tempting response is a grand plan to replace every tool in the business. That can be slower, more expensive and harder to adopt than solving one awkward workflow first.

Start by mapping a single process from beginning to end. For example:

1. Where does the information first arrive?
2. Who changes it, approves it or needs to see it?
3. Where is it copied or re-entered?
4. What happens when something is missing, late or wrong?
5. What does a good outcome look like for the person using the process?

This usually identifies one piece of work worth improving before a larger platform is considered.

## Choose the lightest useful solution

The right answer may be a better template, a clearer process, an existing tool configured properly, an integration between systems or a focused custom application.

Custom software is most useful when the process is particular to the business and the workaround cost has become real: missed time, duplicated work, unreliable data, slow service or decisions made without the information people need.

It should fit the work as it is actually done. It should not require a team to bend around a generic system simply because that system was easier to buy.

## Build in visibility and ownership

When a workflow is important, the people responsible for it should be able to understand what is happening. That can mean clear statuses, sensible permissions, reliable records, useful alerts and a way to recover when something goes wrong.

It also means deciding who will own the process after the initial build. Good software is not only something that works on launch day; it should remain understandable enough to improve as the business changes.
