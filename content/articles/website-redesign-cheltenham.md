# Website Redesign in Cheltenham: What to Keep, Fix or Rebuild

A website can feel tired for several different reasons. The design may no longer reflect the business, the content may be out of date, or customers may struggle to complete a simple task. Those problems do not always require the same solution.

Before commissioning a website redesign in Cheltenham, identify what is failing and what is already useful. That makes it easier to decide whether you need focused improvements, a substantial redesign or a new build.

The aim is to give customers a clearer route to the right information and action, while preserving the things your business still relies on.

## Describe the problem in observable terms

Try to turn a broad concern into something you can inspect. "It looks old" may mean the photographs no longer match the business. "It does not work" might mean a broken form, unclear service descriptions or a layout that is awkward on a phone.

Gather a small amount of useful evidence:

- Repeated questions customers ask after viewing the site.
- Examples of pages with outdated or missing information.
- Tasks that are difficult on a phone.
- Enquiry or booking routes that fail when tested.
- Content your team struggles to update.
- Existing search and traffic information, where available.

You do not need a perfect measurement system to identify obvious problems. Equally, avoid treating a guessed cause as a confirmed one.

## Make an inventory before choosing a new design

List the current pages and mark what should be retained, rewritten, combined or removed. Include downloadable documents, forms, booking links and other features that may sit outside the main navigation.

Record the addresses of important pages. A replacement site may use a different structure, but existing links should receive deliberate treatment rather than simply being forgotten.

Ask who owns the domain, hosting and content, and whether you can access the required accounts. Existing photographs, source files and previous work may be useful if you have the right to use them.

## Separate content problems from layout problems

A fresh colour palette cannot resolve a service description that leaves customers uncertain about what you do. Equally, excellent text can be difficult to use when headings, spacing and navigation obscure the important parts.

Review the main service pages using four questions: what is offered, who is it for, what makes the business credible, and what should the visitor do next?

Then inspect the presentation. Is the main action visible? Does the phone layout maintain the meaning and order of the content? Are sections labelled clearly enough to scan?

This distinction helps direct the budget. Some sites need better copy and a handful of layout changes. Others need a more substantial change to the way content is organised.

## Choose between improvement, redesign and rebuild

A focused improvement is useful when the underlying site is manageable and the problems are limited. Examples include correcting navigation, rewriting a key page or repairing an enquiry form.

A redesign changes the presentation and often the content structure while retaining parts of the existing setup. It may suit a business whose offer has matured but whose website is still maintainable.

A rebuild becomes more relevant when the current system makes necessary changes difficult, lacks required capabilities or cannot be maintained sensibly. Ask the developer to explain that assessment in terms you can verify. A preference for a different tool is not, by itself, a complete business case.

## An illustrative triage exercise

Imagine a Cheltenham professional service business with a functioning website, an old homepage message and a buried contact link. This is a hypothetical example.

The first inspection finds that the main services are still accurate and easy to update. There is no clear reason to replace the whole system. A first phase could revise the homepage, clarify the navigation and test the enquiry journey.

Now imagine the same business also needs customer accounts, but the existing site only presents static information. That requirement deserves a separate technical assessment and scope. It should not be smuggled into a visual refresh.

For your own site, sort each issue into customer information, presentation, functionality or maintenance. Give it a practical consequence and a proposed priority.

## Plan continuity during the change

Before launch, agree how old content and addresses will be handled. If page addresses change, map each important old address to its relevant replacement and arrange permanent redirects. Test those old links after launch, including links from other sites. [Google’s migration guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) describes this preparation.

Test contact routes too, and confirm that business email will continue to work when any domain settings change.

Decide what will happen if the new site has a launch problem. Keeping a recovery route is easier when it is planned in advance.

The [website project timeline](../website-project-timeline/) explains where content approval, access and launch checks fit. A redesign often takes coordination because the existing site is still doing a job while the replacement is being prepared.

## Decide what success will look like

Use concrete outcomes: visitors can identify the right service, the enquiry form works, current examples are visible, and routine updates have an owner. Agree which checks establish those outcomes.

A redesign can support better discoverability, but a new appearance alone does not establish that search visibility or enquiries will improve. Keep the expected benefits tied to the problems being addressed.
