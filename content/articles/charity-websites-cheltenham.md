# Charity Websites in Cheltenham: Clear Routes for Supporters, Volunteers and People Seeking Help

A charity website can have several audiences with very different needs. Someone seeking help may need a calm explanation of what is available. A volunteer wants to know what they would do and whether the time commitment fits. A donor needs to understand the work and how to contribute.

For a Cheltenham charity or community group, a useful website gives these visitors distinct routes. Making every page lead to the same general contact form leaves people unsure what to ask and creates avoidable sorting work for the team.

## Begin with the person who needs support

Explain the support you actually provide, who it is intended for and any limits on availability or area served. Use language that someone unfamiliar with your organisation can understand.

If access is by referral, say who can refer and what the next step involves. If someone can contact you directly, make the relevant route easy to find. State the hours in which messages are handled and avoid suggesting continuous availability when the team cannot provide it.

Separate routine enquiries from any urgent guidance your organisation has approved. Do not improvise health, legal or emergency advice as website filler. The people responsible for the service should review that content.

Ask only for information needed for the initial step. A general contact form should not encourage a person to send a detailed sensitive history without an appropriate process for receiving and handling it.

## Give volunteering its own practical explanation

"Get involved" can be an inviting heading, but it should lead to specific information. Describe current roles, the kinds of tasks involved, the expected commitment and the process for expressing interest.

Some people can offer a regular afternoon; others can help with an occasional event. If both are useful, distinguish them. If no roles are currently available, say so and explain whether you maintain an interest list.

Keep the follow-up manageable. Collect enough information to direct the enquiry, identify who reviews it and explain what a prospective volunteer should expect next. An unattended form can undo the goodwill created by the rest of the page.

## Explain the work before asking for a donation

A donation page should make the organisation and purpose clear. Use accurate descriptions of activities and publish figures only when you can substantiate and explain them.

Real stories can be powerful, but they require care and appropriate permission. Do not make someone receiving support identifiable simply to make a page more persuasive. An anonymised account still needs an accurate and responsible presentation.

Where an established donation provider already works for the organisation, the website can link to that process clearly. Explain any handover so the supporter knows why they are moving to another site. Test the route, the destination identity and the confirmation experience.

## Example: three visitors arriving at one homepage

Imagine a Cheltenham community organisation that offers scheduled practical support and runs volunteer-led sessions. This is a hypothetical planning example.

One visitor selects "Find support" and sees what is available, the area served and how to make contact. A second selects "Volunteer" and reads two current roles with different commitments. A third selects "Donate" and sees a clear explanation of the activity their contribution supports before opening the donation provider.

Each route has a named owner within the organisation. The website reduces uncertainty because it respects the differences between those intentions, not because it collects more information from everyone.

## Design for a small team to maintain

The content plan should match the capacity available after launch. A frequently updated news area is not useful if it stops after the first month.

Decide which pages need regular review: support availability, contact details, current roles, events and any financial or organisational information you publish. An occasional scheduled review can be more realistic than a commitment to constant posting.

Ask the developer to demonstrate ordinary updates with the people who will make them. Write down who controls the domain, hosting and donation account so a change of staff or volunteers does not leave the organisation without access.

## Check the most important journeys

Use this checklist with someone unfamiliar with the organisation:

- Find available support and explain who can access it.
- Identify the next step without relying on internal terminology.
- Find a volunteer role and its likely commitment.
- Follow the donation link and confirm the destination is correct.
- Submit a test enquiry and check who receives it.
- Read key pages on a phone and navigate forms using a keyboard.
- Confirm that someone owns each page requiring regular updates.
